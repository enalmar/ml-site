Research programme

Interfaces decide whether a structure survives an impact.

Modern aircraft, vehicles and protective equipment are built from heterogeneous materials: carbon-fibre laminates, adhesively bonded metal-to-composite joints, additively manufactured parts. Every one of them contains interfaces, and under bird strike, a crash or a blow to the head it is most commonly the interface that fails first. My Royal Academy of Engineering Fellowship, Unveiling Interface Dynamics: Empowering Structural Response in Extreme Environments, builds the experimental and computational tools to predict that failure across length scales and loading rates, so that lighter, more sustainable structures can be certified with confidence.

From quasi-static to shock

A fan-blade-off event loads material roughly a million times faster than a standard tensile test. Properties measured at one rate do not necessarily transfer to another, so my group characterises materials across a range of rates with the same specimen geometry, and builds models that hold at every point on it.

Predicting how bonded joints fail at speed

Bonded joints replace rivets and save weight, but their strength changes with loading rate, bondline thickness and temperature. I developed the methods that measure the full traction–separation response of aerospace adhesives from quasi-static to impact rates, linked the thickness effect to micro-voids seen in X-ray CT, and ran the first dynamic fracture experiments on carbon-fibre-to-titanium joints, which revealed that failure switches from the adhesive to the composite as the rate rises.

Delamination and damage in carbon-fibre laminates

Engine casings must contain high-speed debris; fan blades must survive bird strike. At impact rates the load can't be reliably measured, so the delamination fracture energy is hard to determine. Using the load-free optical method I developed, and working with Rolls-Royce and Innovate UK, we showed that aerospace carbon-fibre laminates are 56–66% tougher against delamination at impact rates than in a standard quasi-static test, with fractography tracing the rise to a change in failure mechanism.

Printed materials for protection

Additive manufacturing lets us design the interior of a material, not just its shape. We built a test method that impacts mouthguards at realistic field-hockey energies, rather than the far gentler standard, and showed that a 3D-printed design cut peak force by 10% and impulse by 25% against the thermoformed EVA benchmark. Internal air-cell structures dissipated a further 25% more energy, and a strain-rate material model ties the design back to how the polymer behaves at impact.

From imperfect materials to predictive models

Real parts contain voids, impurities and process-induced variability. The next phase of the fellowship quantifies how designed and accidental heterogeneity combine to govern impact performance, through stochastic defect–property modelling, machine-learning crack detection from X-ray imaging, and vibration-based structural health monitoring of composite blades.
